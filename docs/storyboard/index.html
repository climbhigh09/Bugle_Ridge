Bugle Ridge

Five seasons and an epilogue. Every chapter adds something new to deal with: wolves, grizzlies, other hunters, and tags that say no. The elk get harder too: they stop answering calls, brush blocks your shots, and bulls walk past on a cow tag.

From your playtest · shipped

Three changes, now in the build

All three are in the game now.

"Stalking is a little slow."

Faster where nothing's happening

  • Three paces by distance: hike 60 yd/min when you’re past 150 yd and hidden, sneak 24 yd/min, creep 10 yd/min inside 60 yd.
  • Terrain still slows you: timber 0.8×, shale 0.7×, deadfall 0.5×.
  • The plan’s time estimate uses the same paces, so “≈ 40 min” means 40 minutes.
  • Moving faster makes more noise and draws more attention when you’re seen.
"If a 3 now, needs to be more like 5."

Graphics at 5

  • The screen is now 240×320: a third more pixels each way, with finer sky gradients, grass and timber.
  • Animals are drawn from anatomy: angular elk racks from spike to 7-point, moose brow tines you can count, black and grey wolves, and a thick grizzly.
  • Still to come: weather and walk cycles.
Battery rule stays

Prettier, not hungrier

  • Idle screens still run at 0 fps. Time and frames only run while you’re glassing, stalking or shooting.
  • Backgrounds are painted once per scene and cached. Only sprites redraw.
  • Mostly near-black at night. Still to come: a sun-glare contrast option for bright days.
  • Updates come from GitHub on launch, with an offline copy for the hill. No other network use.

Difficulty ramp

Eight dials, turned up chapter by chapter

No chapter turns every dial up at once. Each one leans on two or three, so a season has its own feel instead of just more of everything.

New systems

What gets added to the game

Blocked shots

A clear line to the vitals isn't the same as a clear shot. In Colorado it's introduced as an aspen branch and a lesson, then it gets harder every chapter.

Lane checkAt full draw, twigs and trunks between you and the elk show faintly in the sight. Deflection risk is shown only after the shot.
Arrow arcAt 50+ yd an arrow rises about a foot above your line of sight midway. A branch under your line can still eat the arrow.
Rifle vs brushA twig at 40 yd barely matters to a 175-grain bullet. A trunk does. Brushy draws in Idaho put the choice in front of you.
Waiting for a step"Wait for a better angle" now also means waiting for him to step out of the brush. Every wait risks him walking.

Elk that won't play along

Bulls now come in types, and you learn to read which kind is out there.

Vocal herd bullAnswers everything and won't leave his cows. You pull cows to him, or you go to him.
Silent satelliteComes in without a sound, usually downwind. The first sign is antlers in the timber at 40 yd.
Pressured bullOpening week taught him. Calls make him leave. Only a quiet stalk works.
Wolf-shy herdBunched up in timber, moving constantly, not calling. Glass them at first light or not at all.
Post-rut bullRifle season. He's feeding and bedding, and calling only stops him for a second.

Wolves

Idaho and Montana. Wolves aren't a monster to fight. They change how the elk behave and what the country is like.

Howls at dawnThat basin is blown out for the day. Elk hole up in the dark timber or leave the drainage.
SignTracks and a fresh elk carcass on the trail mean the herd is nearby but spooky.
Your meatLeave a quarter overnight and the pack finds it. Hang it high and go back at first light.
Wolf tagOptional in Idaho. A wolf crossing a park at 300 yd is legal with the tag and a violation without it.

Grizzlies

Montana and Alaska. The danger is at the kill site, not on the stalk.

Hang & returnHang the meat 100+ yd from the carcass. Come back into the wind and glass it before you walk in.
A bear on itBack out and give it up, or wait him out. Your bear spray is on your belt, not in the pack.
CampFood in the bear barrel. In Alaska, an electric fence around camp.

Tags that say no

The tag decides what counts, not what your crosshairs are on. Loosely modeled on real Western rules, simplified for play. This is not a regulations guide.

Camp craft

Camp, water, and meat

Choices made at camp each day, before and after the hunt. Most take one tap, and each one trades comfort, time, or risk.

Pick a campsite

Where you sleep sets how far you walk, how cold you are, and whether the elk know you’re there.

Truck at the trailheadWarm, dry, and a cooler for meat. Every hunt starts with a 2–3 mile hike in the dark.
Spike camp near the elkShort walks at first light. Your noise, smoke and scent can push elk out of the basin.
Creek bottomWater close by. Cold air drains in at night, and your morning scent flows downhill with it.
Timbered benchWarmer and out of the wind. Stay off exposed ridges in September lightning.
Grizzly countryCamp away from the kill, food in a bear barrel, cook downwind of the tent.

Find water

You drink every day. Treating water costs time or fuel. Skipping it is a gamble.

SpringCleanest, and often far from camp. Still treat it.
High creekUsually fine once filtered.
Beaver pondClose and easy, and full of giardia. Filter or boil it.
Wallow or stock tankWhere elk and cattle stand in it. Drink it untreated and it’s the 1-in-8 roll.
TreatmentA filter is quick, tablets take 30 minutes, boiling burns fuel. A broken filter is the Oregon Trail moment.

Take care of the meat

How you break down the animal decides how much of it you eat. Heat is the enemy in archery season.

Gutless, boned outBest in September heat. The meat cools fast and packs light: about 230 lb for a bull, in 3–4 trips. Legal where your unit allows boning out.
Quarters on the boneRequired in some units (Alaska moose). Heavier, and the thick hindquarters hold heat. In warm weather, bone sour can take a ham if you don’t cool it fast.
Gutted, wholeFine in November cold near a road. In September, a day in the sun spoils a lot of the meat.
Hang itGame bags in the shade, on the north side of timber, 100+ yd from the carcass. A creek helps. A sunny meadow or a pile on the ground spoils it.
Spoiled meatHank counts what you lost at the fire. Losing too much counts as waste, and wanton waste ends the season.

Your calls

  • Tag soup sends you back to Colorado archery. By Montana that means replaying two full seasons after each miss. Keep it, or restart at the chapter you failed?
  • Should pack-outs cost the rest of the day or more? Real hunters lose a day to a bull on their back. Right now filling the tag ends the season immediately.
  • Bear spray: flavor only, or should carrying it shorten the lost day after a false charge?
